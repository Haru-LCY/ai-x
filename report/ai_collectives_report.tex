\title{Collective-Aware Router Microarchitecture in gem5 Garnet\\
\large Multicast, express-link bypass, and tensor all-reduce}
\author{Chunyu Liu and Boyan Pu}
\date{September 7, 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
GPU training repeatedly maps gradient tensors and synchronization collectives
onto the network.  We study how preserving this communication structure in
gem5 Garnet changes traffic, latency, and throughput.  The implementation
covers the two mechanisms required by Lab~4 Topic~3---physical bypass links and
one-to-many multicast---and evaluates each against an appropriate baseline.
On 4$\times$4, 162 matched cases show 39.51\% mean internal-link flit
reduction, 3.638$\times$ geometric-mean latency speedup, and 190.89\% mean
throughput improvement relative to replicated unicast.  On 8$\times$8, the
same fanouts show 34.95\% traffic reduction, 3.472$\times$ latency speedup, and
208.34\% throughput improvement across another 162 cases, with no throughput
regressions.  A further 108-case 8$\times$8 scale-out study reaches 75.39\%
traffic reduction at fanout 64.  The ten observed regressions are confined to
low-fanout 4$\times$4 cases and arise from atomic branch synchronization.
The bypass study compares distance-scaled diagonal and stride-2 express-link
arms with offline multi-hop route tables and conservative runtime admission.
Under the packet-averaged traffic-pattern aggregation, diagonal reaches
1.426$\times$ and +4.27\% on 4$\times$4,
1.165$\times$ and +12.90\% on 8$\times$8; stride-2 reaches 1.312$\times$
and +4.37\% on 4$\times$4, and 1.620$\times$ and +39.37\% on 8$\times$8.
The plain-Mesh baseline has fewer links, ports, and buffers, so the comparison
measures the performance of the augmented topology at its stated resource
cost.  A Topic~4 case study maps 15 scaled eight-H100 all-reduce events to
multi-flit collective requests and shortens the simulated replay window by
4.728$\times$ relative to 6,720 serialized scalar-lane rounds, with identical
source and Router-forwarded flit counts.
Code and experimental artifacts are available at
\url{https://github.com/Haru-LCY/ai-x}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large-scale GPU training alternates local computation with global
communication.  During each iteration, GPU ranks produce gradient tensors,
exchange them through collectives such as all-reduce and broadcast, and wait
for the synchronized result before beginning the next step.  As illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:training-network}, this repeated dependency maps structured
tensor operations onto packets and flits in the interconnection network.
Network behavior therefore contributes directly to the synchronization path
of training.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{training_to_network.png}
\caption{GPU training repeatedly maps gradient tensors and collective
synchronization onto the Mesh NoC.  This work studies how the Router datapath
represents and transports that structure.}
\label{fig:training-network}
\end{figure}

NCCL exposes the participant groups and operations associated with broadcast
and all-reduce \cite{nccl-guide}, but a conventional network reduces them to
independent packets and destinations.  The three mechanisms in this report
preserve or exploit structure at complementary levels.  Tree multicast shares
common route prefixes for one-to-many delivery.  Tensor streaming retains an
all-reduce event as a multi-flit collective request rather than serializing its
lanes into thousands of independent rounds.  Express links shorten selected
paths through the underlying Mesh.

This organization also maps directly to the project specification.  Lab~4
Topic~3 requires both Bypass and Multicast, together with appropriate
baselines.  We compare physical express-link bypass with plain Mesh XY and
Router-level tree multicast with replicated unicast on the same Mesh.  Topic~4
includes trace-based traffic, which we address by replaying measured H100
collectives through the tensor datapath.  The evaluation asks:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Can Router-side replication reduce physical multicast traffic without
        changing the delivered destination set?
  \item Do express links still improve latency on 4$\times$4 and 8$\times$8
        after their span-dependent wire delay, added radix, and congestion are
        counted, and how does the result change with scale?
  \item Can a traced all-reduce remain a multi-flit tensor request instead of
        thousands of serialized scalar-lane rounds?
\end{enumerate}

Tree multicast shares common route prefixes and produces its largest traffic
reduction at high fanout.  Atomic fanout couples branch progress and can reduce
throughput when one output is congested.  The multi-hop, pressure-aware bypass
primarily benefits loaded structured traffic.  Event-level tensor packetization
then carries the GPU-training narrative from group delivery to the representation
and execution of complete all-reduce tensors.

Each result uses its corresponding unit: internal-link flit reduction, paired latency
ratio, logical throughput change, or trace window.  We report geometric means
for latency ratios, arithmetic means for additive changes, medians and extrema
for skewed distributions, and the number of regressions.  Three deterministic
seeds are retained as paired observations rather than collapsed before ratio
calculation.

Section~2 introduces the shared Garnet substrate and metrics.  Sections~3 and
4 present multicast and bypass, respectively, each from design and
implementation through validation and evaluation.  Section~5 presents the
trace-driven H100 tensor study.  The final sections discuss cross-mechanism
scope, authorship, and reproducibility.

\section{Background and common methodology}

\subsection{Relation to prior work}

Garnet provides the detailed cycle-level network substrate used here
\cite{garnet}.  Virtual Circuit Tree Multicasting established Router-level
tree replication as an alternative to repeated unicast \cite{vctm}; our
implementation instead uses a compact destination bitmap and atomic branch
allocation.  Express Virtual Channels bypass intermediate
Router pipelines over conventional channels \cite{evc}; our mechanism instead
installs explicit span-2 physical links and therefore pays added port, buffer,
and wire proxies.  For routing safety, the offline oracle applies the
channel-dependency condition of Dally and Seitz \cite{dally-deadlock} to its
generated static paths.  SHARP demonstrates hardware streaming aggregation
over reduction trees \cite{sharp-streaming}; the tensor extension applies the
same broad principle to lane-tagged Garnet packets and evaluates the effect of
preserving event-level request structure.

The implementation therefore covers both Topic~3 mechanisms and adds one
Topic~4 trace-based extension.  The following definitions specify the
baseline and measurement procedure for each comparison.

\subsection{Common experimental methodology}

The implementation is based on gem5 v23.0.0.1 \cite{gem5-simulator} and the
cycle-level Garnet model \cite{garnet}.  Plain Mesh and collective trees use
deterministic X-first XY routing.  Evaluated revisions and source hashes are
listed in Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}.

Each performance comparison is paired: baseline and proposed runs use the
same topology, workload realization, packet size, load, and seed.  Multicast
uses replicated unicast as its baseline, bypass uses plain Mesh XY, and the
tensor study compares scalar-lane and event-level representations of the same
trace.  Mechanism-specific configurations and validation are stated in their
own sections rather than combined into one global parameter table.

Latency is reported as baseline latency divided by proposed latency, so a
value above one indicates a speedup.  Internal-link traffic and throughput are
reported as percentage changes from their paired baselines.  A multicast
request completes only after every selected destination receives the complete
packet; multicast throughput therefore counts completed logical requests per
cycle.  Bypass throughput instead counts completed flits per node per source
cycle.

We use arithmetic means for percentage changes and geometric means for
latency ratios within each directly paired group.  For the bypass headline,
the four packet-size geometric means are averaged arithmetically within each
traffic pattern, and the four traffic-pattern values are then combined
geometrically; Table~\ref{tab:bypass-results} states this order explicitly.
Full result-set locations are documented in
Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}.

\section{Tree multicast}

\subsection{Design and baseline}

The baseline Network Interface injects one physical packet per remote
destination and records a selected local destination directly.
The multicast path injects one packet carrying a 64-bit destination bitmap.
Each Router computes the required pruned-tree child branches from that bitmap
and makes a local copy only where selected.  Head, body, and tail
state is retained per branch.  Fanout is atomic: a flit advances only when all
selected output VCs have credit.  This preserves exact multi-flit delivery,
but it also couples a fast branch to a blocked branch---an important
interpretation of the throughput results below.  The bitmap bounds one
collective domain to 64 Routers.

Figure~\ref{fig:multicast-tree-flow} contrasts replicated unicast with the
shared-prefix tree used by the proposed mechanism.

\subsection{Garnet implementation}

The head flit first computes the pruned-tree children selected by its bitmap.
The Router commits branch state only if every required output has a free VC;
body and tail flits reuse those VCs.  Listing~\ref{lst:multicast} is an excerpt
from the implemented two-phase allocation.  This makes a multi-flit packet
exact and prevents partial branch allocation, but also explains the measured
head-of-line coupling: one blocked branch stalls the whole fanout.

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++,caption={Source excerpt: atomic multicast branch allocation (\texttt{Router.cc}).},label={lst:multicast}]
const int vnet = head_flit->get_vnet();
for (const int destination : destinations) {
    const int outport = collectiveOutportForChild(destination);
    if (outport < 0 || !m_output_unit[outport]->has_free_vc(vnet)) {
        m_network_ptr->recordMulticastCreditStall();
        return false;
    }
}
for (const int destination : destinations) {
    const int outport = collectiveOutportForChild(destination);
    const int outvc = m_output_unit[outport]->select_free_vc(vnet);
    fatal_if(outvc < 0, "Router %d lost multicast VC allocation", m_id);
    m_multicast_branches.push_back({outport, outvc, destination});
}
\end{lstlisting}
\end{minipage}

\subsection{Experimental setup and validation}

For every multicast pair, the baseline and tree runs use the same Mesh,
destination set, 1/4/16-flit packet size, request probability
0.1/0.5/1.0, optional uniform background load 0/0.1, and seed 1, 7, or 17.
The balanced matrix uses source Router 5 on 4$\times$4 and Router 27 on
8$\times$8 with fanouts 4, 8, and 16; the separate 8$\times$8 scale-out adds
fanouts 32 and 64.  Each run uses 20 warm-up, 100 measured, and 20 cooldown
requests.  Destination sets below full fanout are sampled without replacement
and may include the source Router.

Correctness validation covers payload values, delivery sets, flit ordering,
and completion under backpressure.  The functional multicast matrix contains
30 destination/topology cases from 2$\times$2 through 8$\times$8, two
implementations, and four packet sizes: 240 executions, or 120 mode-matched
comparisons.  The performance study contains 324 balanced cross-topology pairs
and 108 additional 8$\times$8 scale-out pairs.
Analysis scripts check pair completeness, finite metrics, delivery metadata,
and run counts before generating aggregate figures.  All 240 executions match
the destination bitmap and flit order without missing or duplicate delivery.

\subsection{Results on a 4-by-4 Mesh}


Tree multicast reduces duplicated physical-link traversal, while its throughput
depends on synchronization among output branches.  We therefore report link
traffic and logical-request throughput separately.

The 4$\times$4 study contains 162 matched cases at fanouts 4, 8, and 16.  Tree
multicast reduces internal-link flits by 39.51\% on average (median 41.18\%,
range 16.67--53.13\%) and achieves 3.638$\times$ geometric-mean latency
speedup (median 3.588$\times$, range 1.200--22.512$\times$).  Mean throughput
improves by 190.89\%, with a median improvement of 121.66\%.

Ten 4$\times$4 cases regress in throughput, all at fanout 4; the worst change
is $-18.24\%$.  These cases occur when longer packets and higher load make one
blocked output hold the other tree branches.  The tree still performs less
link work, but atomic fanout couples its progress to the slowest branch.

\subsection{Results on an 8-by-8 Mesh}

The 8$\times$8 study contains 270 matched cases across fanouts 4, 8, 16, 32,
and 64.  Across this complete set, tree multicast reduces internal-link flits
by 48.53\% on average, achieves 6.331$\times$ geometric-mean latency speedup,
and improves mean throughput by 681.77\%.

The common-fanout subset contains 162 cases at fanouts 4, 8, and 16.  It
reduces internal-link flits by 34.95\% on average (median 29.63\%, range
6.67--52.94\%) and achieves 3.472$\times$ geometric-mean latency speedup
(median 2.667$\times$, range 1.161--20.163$\times$).  Mean throughput improves
by 208.34\%, with a median improvement of 145.39\%.

The 8$\times$8 scale-out study adds 108 cases at fanouts 32 and 64.  Mean
traffic reduction is 62.41\% at fanout 32 and 75.39\% at fanout 64.  Their
geometric-mean latency speedups are 11.25$\times$ and 21.62$\times$, while
mean throughput improves by 895.54\% and 1,888.29\%, respectively.  The larger
groups expose more shared route prefixes, so Router-side replication removes
an increasing fraction of repeated link traversal.

\subsection{Analysis across topology, fanout, and packet size}

Topology size is best compared over the common fanouts 4, 8, and 16.  Under
that matched scope, 4$\times$4 and 8$\times$8 have similar geometric-mean
latency speedups (3.638$\times$ and 3.472$\times$), while 4$\times$4 removes
more link traffic (39.51\% versus 34.95\%) and 8$\times$8 has the larger mean
throughput change (+208.34\% versus +190.89\%).  A fixed fanout occupies a
larger fraction of the smaller Mesh, so its destinations more often share the
same XY prefixes; on the larger Mesh, the replicated-unicast baseline also
pays for longer routes, making source-side copy removal more valuable to
throughput.  The 8$\times$8 full-range aggregate in
Table~\ref{tab:multicast-topology-results} is higher because it additionally
contains fanouts 32 and 64, not because Mesh size alone produces the increase.

Fanout is the dominant structural variable.  On 4$\times$4, increasing fanout
from 4 to 16 raises geometric-mean latency speedup from 1.824$\times$ to
6.892$\times$, link-flit reduction from 26.19\% to 53.12\%, and mean
throughput change from +34.38\% to +383.42\%.  On 8$\times$8, the same trends
continue from fanout 4 to 64: latency speedup rises from 1.861$\times$ to
21.618$\times$, link-flit reduction from 29.13\% to 75.39\%, and throughput
change from +44.57\% to +1,888.29\%.  Replicated unicast injects one physical
packet per destination, including repeated traversal of shared route prefixes;
tree multicast injects one packet and copies it only at branch points.  Larger
fanout therefore increases both the number of source-generated copies removed
and the fraction of link traversal shared by the tree.

Packet size separates physical-work savings from timing effects.  For a fixed
destination tree, baseline and tree link traversals both scale linearly with
the number of flits, so the reduction percentage is unchanged across 1-, 4-,
and 16-flit packets in Figure~\ref{fig:multicast-link-work}.  Latency instead
peaks at 4 flits: over common fanouts, its geometric-mean speedup is
2.414$\times$/5.136$\times$/3.884$\times$ on 4$\times$4 and
1.965$\times$/5.142$\times$/4.141$\times$ on 8$\times$8 for 1/4/16 flits.
One-flit packets offer little repeated payload to amortize, whereas 16-flit
packets keep branch VCs reserved until the tail and make atomic fanout wait
longer for the slowest output; four flits retain substantial sharing without
the full long-packet hold time.  The same reservation effect makes mean
throughput change decrease with packet size: +261.65\%/+169.45\%/+141.57\%
on 4$\times$4 and +276.71\%/+188.30\%/+160.01\% on the common 8$\times$8
fanouts.  Figures~\ref{fig:multicast-scaling}--\ref{fig:multicast-throughput-scaling}
therefore show that fanout controls how much network work can be shared, while
packet length controls how efficiently the atomic tree can realize that
structural saving.

\section{Pressure-aware express-link bypass}

\subsection{Proposed topology and baseline}

The two \texttt{Mesh\_Bypass} families append bidirectional express links to
the ordinary Mesh: a diagonal placement and a dimension-aligned stride-2
placement.  Before simulation, a route-table builder enumerates every
current-Router/destination pair and compares ordinary XY with each legal
express edge over the complete remaining path.  The static stride table repeats
aligned express hops within X and then within Y; the static diagonal table uses
an X$^*\!\rightarrow$diagonal$^*\!\rightarrow$Y$^*$ chain.  An express entry
is retained only when it lowers the modeled suffix cost, and exact-cost ties
remain on ordinary XY.

The selected design uses a distance-scaled wire model: each express-link
latency scales with its Manhattan span.  An ablation also evaluated an
optimistic model in which every express link costs one cycle; that model is
treated only as an upper bound on the value of shortening a path.  The plain
Mesh XY comparator has fewer links, ports, VCs, and buffers, so this is a
workload-matched, added-resource comparison rather than an iso-area,
iso-power, or iso-wire claim.

\subsection{Multi-hop routing and runtime admission}

Topology construction creates two directed Garnet links for every installed
physical shortcut and scales their latency by Manhattan span.  The cost of an
ordinary edge is link latency plus Router latency.  An express edge uses its
configured wire latency plus one Router traversal and is entered in the table
only if it lowers the complete suffix cost.  Listing~\ref{lst:bypass} shows the
stride-2 dynamic-programming recurrence; the diagonal builder applies the same
complete-suffix comparison to its phase-ordered diagonal chain.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python,caption={Source excerpt: cycle-aware multi-hop DOR selection (\texttt{bypass\_oracle.py}).},label={lst:bypass}]
@lru_cache(maxsize=None)
def best_stride_step(current, destination):
    if current == destination:
        return 0, None
    ordinary_next = xy_step(current, destination)
    suffix_cost, _ = best_stride_step(ordinary_next, destination)
    choices = [(ordinary_edge_cost + suffix_cost, 0,
                ordinary_next, "", None)]
    for link in outgoing[current]:
        if not dor_express(link, destination):
            continue
        suffix_cost, _ = best_stride_step(link["dst"], destination)
        cost = int(link["latency"]) + router_latency + suffix_cost
        choices.append((cost, 1, link["dst"], link["name"], link))
    cost, _, _, _, selected = min(choices)
    return cost, selected
\end{lstlisting}

At runtime, each Router looks up its precomputed current-Router/destination
entry; it does not recompute the cycle-cost recurrence.  Ordered Vnets retain
that deterministic entry.  For unordered traffic, the selector chooses between
the table's express hop and monotonic XY using local free VCs and credits, the
landing Router's escape/lookahead pressure, and destination-hotspot state.  The
destination-skew detector evaluates epochs of $4N$ data packets, enters the hot
state at four times the uniform share, and retains it until the share falls
below twice uniform.

Admission is also packet-aware.  Diagonal 1-flit packets, and diagonal 4-flit
packets on 4$\times$4, use at most one express hop before continuing on XY.
Packets longer than 32 flits use the full conservative test: the express output
must be at least as healthy as XY, the selected landing lookahead must be idle,
and an entirely idle XY alternative remains on XY.  These rules are part of
the evaluated diagonal and stride-2 arms.

\subsection{Routing safety and validation}

The builder materializes every source--destination path, adds every successive
channel pair to a dependency graph, and requires a topological ordering over
the entire graph.  A cycle formed across multiple routes therefore fails
construction.  Stride runtime adaptation can choose only the table's express
edge or monotonic XY; both strictly approach the destination, complete X before
Y, and never reverse dimension.  Because diagonal packet-aware fallback may
leave the static diagonal chain early, the builder separately constructs and
audits the complete union of its runtime express-or-XY choices rather than
relying only on the static phase-ordered paths.

Route/dependency, traversal, and multi-flit/backpressure suites pass 5, 8, and
14 cases, respectively.  Each bypass family contains 3,072 runs, or 1,536
matched pairs.  Analysis checks pair completeness, finite metrics, route
metadata, and run counts before aggregation.

\subsection{Experimental setup}

Each pair uses the same traffic pattern, packet size, offered load, seed, and
pre-generated source/destination counts on plain Mesh XY and Mesh\_Bypass.  The
4$\times$4 and 8$\times$8 matrices use uniform random, transpose, bit
complement, and 50\% hotspot traffic; 1/4/16/64-flit packets; 16 offered-flit
points from 0.01 to 3.0 per node/source cycle; and seeds 1, 7, and 17.  Each run
uses 1,000 warm-up, 100,000 drain, 5,000 measurement, and 1,000,000 cooldown
source cycles, giving 768 matched pairs per Mesh size and 1,536 per bypass
family.  Both evaluated arms use data VNet 2 with four buffers per VC and
distance-scaled wires.

\subsection{Analysis across traffic patterns}

Figures~\ref{fig:bypass-latency-pattern} and
\ref{fig:bypass-throughput-pattern} expose whether a placement matches the
geometry of a traffic pattern.  For each latency bar, load and seed are first
combined geometrically within each packet size and the four packet-size values
are then averaged arithmetically.  Each throughput bar is the arithmetic mean
over packet size, offered load, and seed.  The two figures therefore compare
the same matched cases but measure different effects: completion time for
latency and sustained completed-flit rate for throughput.

\noindent\textbf{Uniform random.}
Stride-2 scales more strongly than diagonal.  From 4$\times$4 to 8$\times$8,
its latency speedup rises from 1.436$\times$ to 2.478$\times$ and throughput
improvement from +5.82\% to +33.44\%; diagonal reaches only
1.135$\times$/1.184$\times$ and +2.34\%/+6.23\%.  Random pairs do not
consistently present equal X/Y displacement for a diagonal phase, but many
longer 8$\times$8 routes contain repeated axis-aligned two-hop segments.
Stride-2 can use those segments in both dimensions while retaining monotonic
X-before-Y progress, so the opportunity grows with Mesh diameter.

\noindent\textbf{Transpose.}
Diagonal is the natural match because transposition exchanges coordinates and
frequently creates paired X/Y displacement.  It produces the largest diagonal
latency result, 3.004$\times$ on 4$\times$4, compared with 1.227$\times$ for
stride-2; their throughput improvements are +9.69\% and +3.31\%.  On
8$\times$8, diagonal remains ahead at 1.310$\times$ and +18.69\%, versus
1.147$\times$ and +7.69\% for stride-2.  The latency gain does not scale as
quickly as Mesh size because diagonal wires use distance-scaled latency and
the phase-ordered route can exploit a diagonal hop only while both coordinate
directions remain compatible.

\noindent\textbf{Bit complement.}
Complement traffic creates long paths across both dimensions, favoring
repeated stride hops.  Stride-2 improves from 1.663$\times$/+7.90\% on
4$\times$4 to 2.376$\times$/+114.24\% on 8$\times$8.  Diagonal reaches
1.208$\times$/+4.84\% and 1.169$\times$/+25.78\%, respectively.  The larger
Mesh supplies more legal stride-2 entries along a route, reducing Router
traversals repeatedly rather than relying on a single geometric alignment.
Because bit-complement destinations remain distributed, these savings also
relieve network contention instead of merely moving it to one landing Router.

\noindent\textbf{Hotspot.}
All four topology/size combinations remain close to Mesh XY: latency speedup
ranges from 1.004$\times$ to 1.020$\times$, and throughput improvement from
+0.19\% to +2.12\%.  Half of the traffic converges on one destination, so the
last links, landing Router, and destination service rate remain shared
bottlenecks regardless of the earlier shortcut.  Runtime admission responds
to this structure directly: landing pressure and hotspot state suppress an
express hop when it would deliver packets faster into the same congested
endpoint.

Across the four patterns, diagonal is therefore specialized: its clearest
latency advantage appears when traffic follows its geometric phase.  Stride-2
is broader and scales with path length, which explains why its 8$\times$8
aggregate reaches 1.620$\times$ latency speedup and +39.37\% throughput.  The
figures also show why latency and throughput must both be reported.  A shortcut
can shorten an individual route, as in 4$\times$4 transpose, while throughput
remains bounded by shared outputs; when shortcuts are reusable and destinations
remain distributed, as in 8$\times$8 bit complement, both completion time and
sustained rate improve strongly.

\section{Trace-driven H100 tensor all-reduce (Topic~4 extension)}

The synthetic experiments above isolate network mechanisms; this trace-based
Topic~4 study reconnects them to the GPU training loop in
Figure~\ref{fig:training-network}.  It preserves the ordering and tensor sizes
of measured collectives while comparing scalar-lane and event-level request
representations.  In this way, the case study extends the multicast question
from one-to-many delivery to the reduce-and-broadcast structure of all-reduce.

\subsection{Tensor datapath implementation}

Tensor packets retain their multi-flit boundary while each flit carries a lane
identifier.  A collective flit is intercepted before ordinary input-VC
insertion, route computation, switch allocation, and crossbar traversal.  The
Router collective state machine accumulates each
$(\text{collective},\text{lane})$ key and forwards the completed value through
a queued reduce/broadcast path.  Completed lanes wait when the next output VC
is unavailable, so backpressure does not discard reduction state.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++,caption={Abridged source excerpt: lane-wise tensor accumulation (\texttt{Router.cc}).},label={lst:tensor}]
const int lane = t_flit->get_lane_id();
const auto key = std::make_pair(t_flit->get_collective_id(), lane);
CollectiveLaneState& state = m_collective_lanes[key];
state.sum += t_flit->get_value();
++state.count;
if (state.count == m_collective_expected_fanin) {
    const int64_t sum = state.sum;
    m_collective_lanes.erase(key);
    if (m_collective_root)
        forwardCollectiveLane(sum, lane, CollectiveOp::Broadcast,
                              m_id, t_flit);
    else
        forwardCollectiveLane(sum, lane, CollectiveOp::Reduce,
                              parent, t_flit);
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Trace lowering and experimental setup}

The input comes from an eight-H100 80GB HBM3 NCCL microbenchmark
(PyTorch 2.8.0+cu128, CUDA 12.8, NCCL 2.27.3).  The replay compiler selects
15 measurement-phase broadcasts and 15 all-reduces: five repetitions each at
1, 4, and 16~MiB.  It represents communication with 16-byte flits and scales
payload bytes and relative release times by $1/1024$.  An all-reduce event is
therefore 1, 4, or 16~KiB in the replay, corresponding to 64, 256, or 1,024
flits per rank and 6,720 lanes per rank across all 15 events.  Scaling preserves
event ordering and produces a tractable Garnet replay; the reported times are
simulation ticks rather than native H100 or NVLink timings.

For broadcast, the compiler splits events into variable-length tree-multicast
packets.  For all-reduce, scalar lowering repeats each event's release cycle
for $N$ independent one-flit collective rounds.  Tensor lowering instead
creates one $N$-flit packet per event and rank, with a lane ID on every flit.
Both execute the same lane-wise Router reduction followed by tree broadcast.
The JSON records chunks of at most 4~KiB after scaling.  The Garnet loader
combines these chunks into each event's total \texttt{tensor\_flits} and executes
one logical tensor request per event.  The scalar and tensor paths are distinct
from bitmap-based \texttt{tree\_multicast}, although all three use shared tree
prefixes.

Figure~\ref{fig:tensor-allreduce-pipeline} connects the exact trace sizes,
the two lowerings, the Router operation, and the two controlled outcomes.

\subsection{Correctness and backpressure validation}

Fourteen functional cases verify exact lane counts and reduced values across
varied meshes, and 32 backpressure cases vary link latency, Router latency,
and occupancy while requiring completion without residual accumulator state.
Twelve trace-tool unit tests check schema, event sizes and order, collective
IDs, and participants.  In the evaluated replay, both all-reduce
representations complete the same 53,760 rank-lane deliveries and 147,840

\section{Conclusion}

The implementation satisfies both microarchitecture requirements in Lab~4
Topic~3: physical links bypass intermediate Routers, and Router-side
replication sends one packet to multiple destinations.  Each mechanism is
evaluated against an explicit baseline under matched workloads.  On
4$\times$4, tree multicast removes 39.51\% of internal-link flits and achieves
3.638$\times$ geometric-mean latency speedup across 162 matched cases; its ten
throughput regressions all occur at fanout 4.  On 8$\times$8, the common-fanout
matrix removes 34.95\% of link traffic, achieves 3.472$\times$ latency
speedup, and has no throughput regressions across another 162 cases.  The
separate 8$\times$8 scale-out study reaches 75.39\% traffic reduction at
fanout 64.  This separation identifies atomic branch synchronization as a
4$\times$4 low-fanout effect rather than a general multicast trend.  The
latency results reflect both shared-prefix replication and the dedicated
  Router datapath.  The bypass design builds a complete-suffix cycle-cost table
  offline; each Router looks up that table and admits distance-scaled express
  hops under packet-aware local, landing, and hotspot pressure rules.  The
  aggregate bypass result is
  1.426$\times$/+4.27\% for diagonal on 4$\times$4,
  1.165$\times$/+12.90\% for diagonal on 8$\times$8,
  1.312$\times$/+4.37\% for stride-2 on 4$\times$4, and
  1.620$\times$/+39.37\% for stride-2 on 8$\times$8.  Mesh\_Bypass adds links,
  ports, and buffers, making this an added-resource
comparison with explicit topology-specific cost proxies.  The H100 trace
then extends the collective study to complete tensors: preserving 15
event-level multi-flit all-reduce requests shortens the scaled replay window by
4.728$\times$ with identical counted network work.

Together, the experiments show how exposing communication structure to the
network removes duplicated transport and unnecessary request scheduling.
Tree replication shares multicast prefixes, event-level tensor packets avoid
scalar-lane serialization, and pressure-aware routing directs express traffic
according to modeled cycle cost and current congestion.
